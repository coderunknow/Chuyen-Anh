While this simulation looks much further ahead than most conventional climate models, the researchers warn that this extreme scenario is fast becoming inevitable unless governments worldwide take drastic and immediate action to limit greenhouse gas emissions.

B. According to the Princeton team, rising CO2 levels will trigger higher temperatures not only at the Earth’s surface, but also deep within the troposphere. By factoring this atmospheric warming into their models, alongside projected changes to levels of water vapour, cloud cover, solar radiation, and ozone, the team predicted the precise effects that climate change would have on global evaporation and precipitation. The researchers discovered that both elements would increase, consequently causing the total discharge of fresh water from rivers around the world to rise by almost 15 per cent. However, while water is going to be more plentiful in regions that already possess an abundance, the net effect of this shift will be to take the world’s water further away from where human populations are actually concentrated.

C. “Water stresses will increase significantly in regions that are already relatively dry,” Manabe reports in the journal Climate Change. Evaporation will severely reduce the moisture content of soils in many semi-arid parts of the world, including north-east China, the grasslands of Africa, the Mediterranean, and the southern and western coasts of Australia. For example, soil moisture is projected to fall by up to 40 per cent in the southern states of the United States.

D. The effects on the world’s rivers will be just as dramatic, but highly unequal. The most substantial increases in river flow will occur in the thinly populated tropics and the far north of Canada and Russia. For instance, the flow of the river Ob in Siberia is projected to increase by 42 per cent by the end of the 23rd century. This particular prediction could encourage Russia’s historical plans to divert Siberian rivers in order to irrigate the arid deserts surrounding the Aral Sea. Similarly, such ecological changes could increase political pressure from the US for Canada to allow water transfers from its giant Pacific rivers to water the increasingly parched American West. Manabe’s model also predicts a 47 per cent increase in the flow of the Yukon river.

E. By contrast, there will be significantly lower flows in many mid-latitude rivers which run through heavily populated regions. Those that will start to decline include the Mississippi, the Mekong, and especially the Nile - one of the world’s most heavily utilized and politically contested rivers - where the Princeton model predicts an 18 per cent fall in water flow. These massive geographical shifts will present a “profound challenge” to the world’s water managers, Manabe says. They are also highly likely to fuel global calls for a new generation of engineering projects, such as super-dams and massive canals to move water round the planet, mirroring China’s current scheme to transfer water between its north and south.

F. Some of the team’s findings, however, remain controversial within the scientific community. The UK Met Office’s own climate model predicts that water flows in the Amazon could actually fall during this century, whereas Manabe’s team predicts greater rainfall could increase its flow by 23 per cent. Furthermore, while Manabe foresees a 49 per cent increase in the flow of the Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers that drain the Himalayas, a separate international study reported that the Ganges would eventually lose its flow as the mountain glaciers that feed it melt away entirely.

G. Meanwhile, a team of researchers in France argue that climate change is not just a future threat, but is already actively affecting the world’s rivers today. David Labat and his colleagues at the government’s CNRS research agency in Toulouse painstakingly reconstructed the monthly discharges of more than 200 of the world’s largest rivers dating back to 1875. They utilized historical discharge data held by the Global Runoff Data Centre in Germany and the UNESCO River Discharge Database, applying a specialized statistical technique to fill in historical gaps left by missing data or artificial changes to run-off caused by human dams and irrigation projects. Their findings reveal that changing global temperatures cause river flows to rise and fall after a specific time delay of about 15 years. The French team predicts that global river flows will increase by about 4 per cent for every 1°C rise in global temperature. However, climate change over the past few decades has already caused modern discharge from rivers in North America, South America, and Asia to increase. Conversely, while run-off in Europe has remained relatively stable, the flow of water from Africa’s rivers has already suffered a noticeable fall.
