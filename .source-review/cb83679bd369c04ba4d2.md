A genetically modified organism (GMO) is created when scientists insert genetic material from one organism into another in order to transfer a desirable trait. This makes GMOs inherently “unnatural”, since such combinations would not occur through ordinary reproduction without human intervention. As a result, genetic engineering places scientists in a creative role that many people find troubling: some believe such control over living things should remain beyond human authority, while others fear that science may make irreversible mistakes. Among the most persistent concerns is that artificial combinations of traits could create dangerous new organisms capable of harming ecosystems. Another widespread fear is that genetically modified food products may ultimately be found harmful to human health.

These concerns became the basis for serious opposition to genetically modified food technology, particularly from environmental organizations in Europe. Ironically, GM crops were originally developed partly for ecological reasons: by making plants resistant to pests, scientists hoped to reduce the use of chemical pesticides and thus lessen environmental contamination. Nevertheless, opposition intensified in the late 1990s after British scientist Dr. Arpad Pusztai suggested that genetically modified potatoes might be linked to immune-system abnormalities in laboratory rats. At the same time, aggressive promotion of GM crops by major American corporations further fueled controversy. Conflicting scientific claims left the public uncertain, and in response the European Union imposed a de facto moratorium on new GM food approvals in 1998.

Companies heavily involved in the GM food market came under substantial pressure at the turn of the millennium, not only from environmental campaigners and consumer groups, but also from shareholders. [I] They feared that the risks surrounding GM food might reduce the value of their investments. [II] One major concern was that companies could face enormous legal liability if a GM product was eventually shown to cause health problems. [III] These shareholder coalitions introduced resolutions at corporate meetings to limit exposure to risk. Some called for a moratorium on the purchase of GM ingredients until safety was more convincingly demonstrated. Others focused on mandatory labeling so that consumers would know whether a product contained genetically modified components. [IV]

In most markets, however, prospects for the acceptance of GM food as safe and commercially practical improved during the early years of the new century. The European moratorium was partially relaxed in 2003, as most studies up to that point showed no conclusive evidence of harmful effects in countries across the Americas, where no similar ban existed. In 2006, the World Trade Organization ruled that the European restrictions were inconsistent with international trade obligations, making similar long-term suspensions less likely. By that same year, more than one billion cumulative acres worldwide had been planted with genetically modified crops. As demand for conventional seeds weakened, more firms competed for a share of the multibillion-dollar agricultural biotechnology market. Health concerns also subsided somewhat, largely because years of scientific studies had failed to reveal any striking evidence of danger. In practical dietary terms, replacing conventionally grown potatoes with genetically modified ones appeared to produce no remarkable effects.

Even so, opposition to GM food has remained strong on philosophical and ethical grounds. Much recent criticism has centered on the patenting practices of companies that develop genetically modified plants and on the degree of power such patents confer. After investing heavily in research, biotechnology firms seek to market their products without competition from companies that have not made similar investments. This raises the question of whether a living organism should be treated as patentable property at all. In many countries, the law has answered yes. To preserve exclusive control, some companies have introduced biological mechanisms that prevent farmers from freely reproducing patented crops. Certain modified seed varieties are engineered so that harvested seeds cannot reliably be replanted for the following year’s crop. Unlike conventional plants, therefore, they force farmers to return each season to the patent holder for fresh seed stock. In this sense, the company becomes not merely a supplier, but the continuing gatekeeper of agricultural life.
